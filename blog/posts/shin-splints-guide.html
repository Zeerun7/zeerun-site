Shin Splints: The Runner's Complete Guide

September 25, 2026 · 6 min read · By the ZeeRun team

You know the feeling: three or four minutes into a run, a dull ache spreads along the front of your shin. It eases if you stop, returns when you start, and by the end of the week it's greeting you before you've left the driveway. That's shin splints — medically, medial tibial stress syndrome — and it's the single most common complaint among runners in their first year.

The good news: it's one of the most fixable problems in running. The bad news: most runners fix it wrong, by either ignoring it until it becomes a stress fracture or resting completely and losing months of fitness. Here's the middle path.

What shin splints actually are

Despite the name, it's rarely the bone itself at first. The pain comes from overloaded tissue where your calf muscles attach along the tibia — the periosteum gets irritated when the load on your lower leg exceeds what it's currently conditioned to handle. Think of it as your shin's way of filing a complaint about your training, not a structural failure.

That distinction matters, because it tells you the fix isn't just rest — it's recalibrated load.

What usually causes them

In our experience, it's almost always one (or more) of these four:

  • Too much, too soon. The classic. Weekly mileage jumps of 20–30%, a sudden return after time off, or adding speed work on top of rising volume. Your cardiovascular fitness adapts faster than your bones and connective tissue — your lungs are ready for week six while your shins are still on week two.
  • Worn-out shoes. Cushioning and support degrade long before the upper looks tired. If your daily trainers have 400+ miles on them, they're suspects.
  • Hard, cambered surfaces. Concrete sidewalks and slanted roads concentrate impact. Trails and tracks are kinder while you're rebuilding.
  • Weak calves and tibialis anterior. The muscles that should be absorbing shock aren't pulling their weight, so the bone takes the hit instead.

The 10% rule (and when to break it)

The old rule — increase weekly mileage by no more than 10% — is conservative, but conservative is exactly what irritated shins need. If you're at 15 miles a week, next week is 16–17, not 22. Add a "down week" every third or fourth week where volume drops ~20% to let tissue catch up.

But here's the nuance the rule misses: 10% of what? If your shins are already barking, 10% more of an already-too-much load is still too much. The real rule is simpler — pain should not be getting worse week over week. Stable or improving means your load is about right. Trending upward means cut volume now, not after one more long run.

What to do this week

If your shins are talking to you right now:

  1. Cut volume, keep it easy. Drop mileage 30–50% for a week or two. All easy pace — no intervals, no strides, no "testing it" with a tempo.
  2. Ice after runs. 10–15 minutes on the sore area calms the irritation. It's symptom management, not a cure, but it keeps things from spiraling while you fix the cause.
  3. Strengthen the area. Twice a week: toe walks, heel walks, and tibialis raises (stand with your back to a wall, lift your toes toward your shins, 3×15). Add eccentric calf raises off a step — the single best shin-splint exercise in the research literature.
  4. Soften the surface. Move easy runs to a track, trail, or treadmill until the ache settles.
  5. Check your shoes. Honest mileage count. If they're past their prime, retire them.

Niggle or stop signal? The test

This is the question every runner gets wrong. Here's a practical framework:

  • Modify (keep running easy): dull ache that appears a few minutes in, doesn't worsen during the run, and is gone or unchanged the next morning. Stable is manageable.
  • Stop (take days off): sharp or localized pain you can point to with one finger, pain that worsens during the run, limping, or pain that's worse the morning after. One-finger pinpoint pain on the bone itself is a red flag — that can be a stress fracture, and it needs a clinician, not an app.
Stable and not worsening means go easy and check in after mile one. Anything trending upward means bagging it. Your season will thank you.

Coming back

Once you've had a few pain-free days, don't jump back to full volume. Rebuild with walk-run intervals on soft surfaces, increasing the running portions over 1–2 weeks. Your fitness barely dented in that time — your shins just needed the load curve to match their conditioning.
