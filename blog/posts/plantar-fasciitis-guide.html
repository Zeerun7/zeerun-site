Plantar Fasciitis: A Runner's Guide to Heel Pain

October 4, 2026 · 6 min read · By the ZeeRun team

You know it the moment your feet hit the floor. That first step out of bed feels like stepping on a nail — a sharp stab deep in the heel. You hobble to the bathroom, it eases a little, and by mid-run it might even disappear. Then it comes roaring back that evening.

That's plantar fasciitis, the most common cause of heel pain in runners — and one of the most misunderstood niggles in the sport. The good news: most runners beat it without ever fully stopping. The bad news: the instinct to either "run through it" or "rest until it's gone" both tend to backfire. Here's the middle path that actually works.

What it actually is

The plantar fascia is a thick band of tissue running along the bottom of your foot, from heel to toes. It acts like a bowstring, supporting your arch and absorbing load every time you land. When that load exceeds what the tissue can handle — usually because you ramped mileage too fast, switched to a harder surface, or your shoes gave out — it becomes irritated where it attaches to the heel bone.

Despite the "-itis," this is usually a degenerative overload problem, not an infection or simple inflammation. That's an important distinction: it means rest alone rarely cures it. The tissue needs progressive, tolerable loading to remodel and get stronger — which is exactly why so many runners come back worse after six weeks of doing nothing.

The classic pattern

Plantar fasciitis has a signature feel, and knowing it saves you from weeks of misdiagnosis:

  • First-step pain. Worst in the morning or after sitting. A few steps in, it warms up.
  • Post-run ache. It often feels okay during the run, then flares for hours afterward.
  • Press-and-hurt spot. Tenderness on the inside of the heel, right where the arch starts.

Sharp pain that shoots up the leg, numbness, or swelling that doesn't ease up over a few days — those are different problems. See a clinician rather than self-managing those.

What to do this week

This is a niggle, not an emergency — but it rewards prompt, sensible action. Here's the plan for week one:

1. Roll your arch, daily

Two minutes per foot on a lacrosse ball or golf ball. Work slowly from heel to toes. Do it before your run and before bed. If you have a frozen water bottle, rolling on that doubles as icing — one of the few free treatments with genuinely good evidence behind it.

2. Start calf raises

Tight calves are plantar fasciitis's usual accomplice. Begin with both-leg calf raises on flat ground — 3 sets of 12, every other day. Over weeks, progress to single-leg raises, then to raises off a step with a slow lower. This is the exercise with the best track record for stubborn cases.

3. Cut volume, not running

Drop your weekly mileage 30–50% and swap intensity for easy running. Keep it flat: hills, speedwork, and long runs all load the fascia hardest. If your heel hurts more than a 3 out of 10 during the run — or if morning first-step pain is worse the next day — you overshot. That's your dosage meter.

4. Audit your shoes

Worn-out cushioning and a sudden switch to low-drop or minimalist shoes are both classic triggers. You don't need magic footwear, but check the mileage on your current pair and avoid racing flats until the heel is happy.

The 24-hour rule

It's okay to run through mild plantar fascia pain (3/10 or less during the run) — if your first-step pain the next morning is no worse than yesterday. If morning pain climbs, you ran too hard. That's the rule that separates healing from grinding.

What not to do

Don't inject cortisone and declare victory — relief without fixing the load problem just buys you a bigger relapse. Don't wear rigid night splints as a first resort; the calf raises matter more. And don't "rest until it's 100% gone" for months: plantar fasciitis loves a deconditioned runner, and the comeback from total rest is often when things get worse.

When to get help

If there's no improvement after 3–4 weeks of consistent self-management — the arch rolling, the calf raises, the volume cut — book a physio who works with runners. You may need progressive loading programmed properly, or a check that something else (a stress reaction in the heel bone, for example) isn't masquerading as fasciitis. Persistent pain deserves a professional eye, not a louder guess.

Keeping it gone

Once the morning hobble is a memory, keep the calf raises in your routine twice a week — forever, ideally. They're cheap insurance. Ramp mileage by no more than ~10% per week, be cautious with back-to-back hard days, and retire shoes before they look tired. Most recurrences come from the same two sins: too much, too soon.

The honest truth about plantar fasciitis is that it's a load-management problem, not a rest problem. The runners who beat it fastest are the ones who keep running — carefully, in measured doses, with the calf raises and the 24-hour rule. Your body is giving you daily feedback; the trick is listening to it instead of arguing with it.
