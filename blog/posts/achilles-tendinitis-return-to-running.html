Achilles Tendinitis: A Runner's Return-to-Running Plan

September 30, 2026 · 8 min read · By the ZeeRun team

A quick but important note: this article is educational information, not medical advice. Achilles problems have several different causes — tendinopathy, bursitis, paratenonitis — and only a clinician who examines you can say what's going on. If your calf suddenly pops or you can't push off your foot, stop and get urgent care. If swelling is increasing, a lump feels like it's growing, or you can't walk without a limp, see a physiotherapist or sports-medicine doctor before following any plan.

It starts with the morning routine you wish you didn't have: swing your legs out of bed, stand up, and take those first few steps like you're eighty. The back of your ankle aches, stiff and grumpy. By mid-morning it loosens up, you convince yourself it's fine, you run, and the next morning it's worse again.

That's the classic Achilles tendinitis pattern — stiff in the morning, better with gentle movement, angry after runs. And the Achilles is the tendon runners most dread coming back from, because rushing it is exactly how weeks of setback turn into months.

The good news: Achilles issues respond well to patient, graded loading. Tendons adapt slowly, but they do adapt. Here's how to come back without the boom-and-bust cycle.

What Achilles tendinitis typically feels like

The following describes patterns many runners report. It's general information, not a diagnosis of your ankle:

  • Stiffness and aching at the back of the heel/ankle, worst first thing in the morning.
  • It eases after a few minutes of gentle movement, then returns or worsens after a run.
  • The tendon may feel thick or lumpy compared to the other side.
  • Pressing on the tendon is tender; pinching it can be genuinely unpleasant.
  • No single moment of injury — it crept in over weeks, often when mileage, hills, or speed increased.

If the tendon ever goes from achy to suddenly sharp, if your calf pops or you lose the ability to push off, that's a different situation — stop and get checked immediately. A ruptured Achilles is urgent.

Why the Achilles is so slow to forgive

Muscles have a rich blood supply and heal quickly. Tendons don't. The Achilles gets far less blood flow, so its repair process runs on a weeks-to-months clock, not a days-to-weeks one.

The other trap: the Achilles handles enormous load. Every running step loads the tendon with several times your body weight. So the gap between "feels fine walking" and "can tolerate running" is big — bigger than for most other running niggles. Walking pain-free is the entry ticket, not the finish line.

This is why "I rested it until it felt okay, then ran my normal route" fails so reliably with the Achilles. Rest calms the irritation; it doesn't rebuild the tendon's capacity. Only gradual, progressive loading does that — strength work first, then walk/run, then hills and speed last.

Before you start: the entry criteria

Don't start a return-to-run plan until these are true. This isn't a test to hero through — missing one just means more calm-down time first:

  1. Morning stiffness is gone or nearly gone. If you're still doing the 80-year-old shuffle, wait.
  2. You can walk 30 minutes with no Achilles pain. Brisk, on flat ground, zero symptoms during or after.
  3. Single-leg heel raises are pain-free. This is the key benchmark: 15 slow single-leg heel raises on the affected side, pain-free or at most 2/10. If that fails, your strength foundation isn't ready.
  4. No growing lump, no increasing swelling. Stable or shrinking is fine; growing is not.
  5. You can hop lightly on the sore leg without sharp pain (the flat-ground running test).

The heel-raise test is the gate

Clinicians use single-leg heel-raise capacity as the practical readiness check for Achilles issues. Fifteen slow, pain-free reps on the sore side is the common bar. Two heel-raise sessions a week, building toward 3×15, should also be a permanent part of your training — tendons need ongoing loading to stay resilient.

The phased return: flat ground first

Achilles comebacks go in strict phases. Hills and speed work come last because the tendon loads hardest on inclines and during fast, springy strides. Here's the progression:

Phase 1 — Walk/run on flat ground (2–3 weeks): Start with 1 min run / 2 min walk × 8, three times a week, never on back-to-back days. Flat, soft surfaces. During the run: Achilles pain ≤3/10. Next morning: stiffness no worse than usual. Advance by extending the run interval a minute or two per week — not by adding hills or speed.

Phase 2 — Continuous easy running (2–3 weeks): Once you can run/walk 30 minutes comfortably, start stitching together continuous runs. Keep them easy and flat. Continue heel-raise strength work twice a week throughout — this is not optional during the comeback.

Phase 3 — Gentle strides and rolling terrain (2 weeks): Add a few relaxed strides (not sprints) once a week and allow gentle rolling hills. If morning stiffness returns, drop back to Phase 2 for a week.

Phase 4 — Full training: Hill reps and speed sessions come back last, one new stress per week, never both at once.

The Achilles-specific rules

Every comeback has ground rules. For the Achilles, three matter most:

  1. Stretching is not rehab for tendons. Aggressive calf stretching on an angry Achilles often makes it worse. Gentle mobility is fine; stop if the stretch provokes lasting soreness. Loading — heel raises and running — is what remodels the tendon.
  2. No hills or speed until Phase 3. Uphill running and fast strides spike tendon load. They are the most common "I was fine until..." story in Achilles comebacks.
  3. The 24-hour test is law. The run itself is a poor judge. The verdict comes the next morning: stiffness back to baseline means the load was fine. Stiffness clearly worse means repeat the current phase for another week.

One more thing to be patient about: mild stiffness at the start of a run that eases within a few minutes is common and generally fine during the early phases. Sharp or increasing pain is not.

Strength work that actually protects the tendon

Tendons need load to stay healthy — carefully dosed, progressive load. The backbone program during and after your comeback:

  • Slow single-leg heel raises: 3×15 on each side, twice a week, off a step so your heel drops below the edge. Pain ≤3/10. Once 3×15 feels easy, add load — a loaded backpack works.
  • Bent-knee heel raises: same protocol with knees slightly bent, to hit the deeper soleus muscle. This one often gets skipped; don't skip it.
  • Heavy, slow calf raises: over time, heavier weights with slower tempos. Tendons adapt to high load applied gradually.

Keep this program going after you're back to full training. Most Achilles recurrences happen because runners stop the strength work the moment they're healthy again.

Red flags — see a clinician instead

  • Sudden pop, severe pain, or inability to push off — urgent, possible rupture.
  • Swelling or a lump that's visibly growing.
  • Pain so sharp you can't walk normally.
  • Numbness, tingling, or color changes in the foot.
  • No meaningful improvement after 6–8 weeks of sensible graded loading.

Also: if the pain is more on the inner ankle bone, the heel, or radiates into the calf rather than the tendon itself, it may not be the Achilles at all — a physio's assessment is worth the visit.

A realistic timeline

Plan on 8–12 weeks from "starting back" to "full training" for a typical first-time Achilles setback, and longer if it keeps flaring. That's not a delay — it's the tendon's actual schedule. Every runner who tried to compress it learned the lesson twice.

The runners who get through cleanly share a pattern: they treated the heel-raise benchmark like a gate, they progressed only when mornings stayed calm, and they kept strength work in the program long after the comeback ended. Boring, patient, effective.
